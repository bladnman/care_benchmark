## 3. Diagnostic patterns

- **Affective vs functional.** Functional whys recovered 30.0 / 48.0 weighted points (62.5%), while affective whys recovered 33.0 / 86.0 (38.4%). The clearest functional survivals are S7, S8, F2, F6, F21, and F36. Affective relationship-framing rows such as F8, F23, F31, F33, F37, and F39 mostly degrade to rules.
- **Weight-3 vs weight-2.** Weight-3 whys recovered 48.0 / 96.0 (50.0%), somewhat better than weight-2 whys at 15.0 / 38.0 (39.5%). High-weight rows often recovered primary mechanics but lost downstream consequences.
- **System-level vs feature-level.** System-level fidelity (75.0%) is much higher than feature-level fidelity (39.6%). The plan carries broad architecture and surface philosophy better than it carries non-obvious per-feature intent.
- **Multi-layer recovery patterns.** Primary implementation layers survived more often than secondary and downstream layers. The most common miss was the consequence layer: why a shortcut would collapse the relationship, corrupt trust, or turn the product into a different one.
- **Subdomain patterns.** Accounts/sync and telemetry performed best because the plan names concrete privacy and canonical-state boundaries. Social and relationship-continuity features were weakest: several social rules exist, but their private-relationship rationale does not.
- **Evidence-bound effects.** 14 included whys were marked rule_without_why. Most denials were not because the feature was absent; they were because B could only reconstruct the operational rule from PLAN.

What the failure shape suggests: this plan is buildable and broad, but it is often an engineering plan rather than an intent-preserving product plan. It would likely produce many correct mechanics while leaving future contributors free to erode charm with reasonable-seeming additions.

---

## 4. Recommendations for v2 hardening

- Add more feature-level exceptions around social, notification, identity-continuity, and account rights. This run shows those rules are easy to implement while dropping the relationship rationale.
- Keep multi-layer whys, especially for high-weight affective rows. Downstream consequence layers were the first to disappear and were diagnostically useful.
- Preserve the v06 evidence-bound operator. It prevented mechanism-only rows from scoring as intent recovery and exposed 14 rule-without-why cases.
- Consider adding a separate "rationale density" diagnostic for phase-1 plans. This candidate captured 101 features, but many entries are API/schema-level rather than why-bearing.
- Keep the system-level cross-cutting bar, but document partial cases like S2 and S6 carefully; a principle can be operationally present while not reconstructed as a principle.

## 5. Methodology caveats

- **Fresh-context fidelity.** The frozen reconstruction reads as plan-derived: it follows PLAN headings, uses PLAN vocabulary, and shows no gold ID leakage or scorer-side rubric vocabulary. Same-context risk appears low from the artifacts available to this scorer.
- **Single-run-at-temperature limitation.** This is one run with no variance signal.
- **Borderline capture calls.** Capture leaned inclusive on mood state, mood persistence, bird-chosen perch, weather-to-mood behavior, social visit notification default, visitor actual-state view, and reduced-motion charm. These calls mostly affect planning quality and reachable denominators, not recovered why credit.
- **System-level cross-cutting.** S2 and S6 were the subjective cases: the plan preserves several operational descendants, but B does not fully name the system principle. They were scored partial.
- **Confabulation cases.** No clear ungrounded reconstruction cases were found. B is more often conservative than confabulatory, marking several items NOT RECOVERABLE FROM PLAN.
- **Evidence-bound denials.** F8, F11, F23, F26, F27, F31, F33, F37, and F39 are representative rows where rule evidence exists but the gold why does not.
- **Operational compromise.** The timing ledger available to this scorer included phase1 and phase2a only; phase2b timing was not present at scoring time.
